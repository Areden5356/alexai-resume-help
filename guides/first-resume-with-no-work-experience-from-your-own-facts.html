A first resume with no work experience, built from your own facts

Updated 21 October 2026

If you have never held a paid job, your resume is not empty. It is made from different material: school, projects, clubs, volunteering and small jobs that nobody put on a payroll. The rule I use is the same one as for every other resume here. Every line must be something you did and could talk about for two minutes. This guide shows how to find those facts, order them, and stop before you pad.

What the sources say

CareerOneStop suggests an order of contact information, optional sections such as a professional summary or skills, then education and training, then work history. Purdue's writing lab says the education section should name your highest degree, the school, its city and state, and the date, and that someone with no college can list high school or a GED. It also says grade point average is optional, and warns that leaving it off may lead a reader to assume it was low. Purdue's experience page says the section can include volunteer, intern, student and leadership roles, not only paid jobs, and that students can add "Skills Learned" beside responsibilities. Its page on honors and activities says to brainstorm a full list, then choose the items most relevant to the job.

Those pages do not tell a first-time applicant exactly what to do. The method below is my judgment, built on those points.

Step 1: brainstorm without judging

Write every answer to these questions on one page, before you decide what to keep.

  • Which classes ended in something you made, wrote or presented?
  • Which clubs, teams or groups did you belong to, and did you hold a role?
  • Where have you volunteered, even for one weekend?
  • What informal work have you done: tutoring, babysitting, yard work, helping at a family business, selling things?
  • What tools can you use, and where did you use each one for real?
  • Which languages do you speak well enough to use?

Step 2: add the facts that make each item specific

For each item that survives, write down how many, how often, how long and for whom. "Tutored" says little. "Tutored two classmates in algebra once a week for a semester" says something an interviewer can ask about. If you do not remember a number, leave it out. Do not estimate to fill a gap.

Step 3: choose by the posting

Read the posting and keep the items that show something it asks for. Drop the rest, even if you are proud of them. Purdue's example is that a youth leadership role may matter little for an engineering job and a lot for a teaching job. A first resume is short, and one page is plenty.

Step 4: put the strongest section first, after education

With no jobs, education usually opens the page. Then put your strongest material next, whether that is projects, activities or volunteering. The projects guide shows how to write a project entry.

Example, invented for illustration 
Jordan Example
Education
  Associate of Arts, expected May 2027
Projects
  Neighborhood Tool Library, class project, Spring 2026
  - Surveyed 30 neighbors about tools they would lend
  - Built a sign-out spreadsheet used by four households
Volunteer
  Food pantry sorter, Saturdays, Fall 2025
  - Sorted donated cans by date and type, about 3 hours a week
Skills
  Spreadsheets (built the sign-out sheet above), Spanish (spoken)

Each line points to something real. The skills line names where each skill was used. A bare list of tools, with no place they were used, is easy to doubt.

Step 5: write bullets that say what you did

Start each with a plain action verb, use the same tense throughout, and say what came of it. The bullets guide has questions that pull out the details, and the free bullet check flags weak openings and vague words.

What to leave out

  • A job title you did not hold. "Marketing Intern" for helping a relative's shop is a claim someone can check.
  • Skill lists you cannot demonstrate. If you cannot show where you used it, drop it or mark it "coursework".
  • Personality adjectives. "Hard-working" and "motivated" cost words and prove nothing. A fact does the work instead.
  • Filler to reach a full page. A short, honest page reads better than a padded one.

What if the page still feels thin?

Say so plainly in a cover letter, and name one thing you can do now. A line such as "I have not held a paid job, and the neighborhood tool library is the best example of my work" is honest and specific. The cover letter guide shows where it fits. If a gap in time is the worry rather than a lack of jobs, see the guide on gaps.
