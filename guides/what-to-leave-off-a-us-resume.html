What to leave off a US resume: photo, age, references, salary

Updated 26 October 2026

Most resume advice is about what to add. A short list of things belongs off the page, and people often include them because a template, a relative or a resume from another country showed them how. This guide collects what two university career offices, MIT and Harvard, say to omit from a resume written for a US employer. Where I add my own reasoning, I say so.

Personal details that are not about the job

MIT tells students not to put age, religion, health or marital status on a resume. Harvard's list says not to include age or gender. Neither page gives a legal explanation, and I will not either, since that question belongs to an employment lawyer. My practical reading: these facts do not show you can do the work, so they give the reader nothing to use and something to react to.

A photo

Harvard says not to include a picture. MIT says photos are generally not preferred on US resumes. Both statements are about the US. Harvard adds that resume rules vary from country to country and points students who plan to work abroad to separate resources. If you apply outside the US, check local norms for that country before copying American habits or leaving out what is expected there.

A references list

Both offices say to leave references off the page. MIT's wording is to line them up but not include them unless asked. It adds that employers assume references are available, so the line "references available upon request" is unnecessary. Keep a separate sheet with each person's name, title, how they know you and their contact details, after asking permission. The reference guide covers who to ask and what to say.

Past salary

MIT says you will typically not be expected to share past salary information on a resume. If a form asks for it, answer what the form asks truthfully. Whether an employer may ask, and how you may answer, differs by place and I cannot advise on it.

Habits of style that Harvard says to drop

  • Personal pronouns such as "I" and "we". Start a line with the action instead.
  • A narrative style. Short fragments are the norm, not full paragraphs.
  • Slang and colloquial wording.
  • Abbreviations, which Harvard lists as a "don't". Spell out the first use, and use your judgment on terms the reader knows.
  • Starting every line with a date. Put the role or employer first so the eye lands on what you did.

MIT agrees on pronouns and adds that resumes do not need complete sentences. Both want a bullet to begin with a strong verb. The bullets guide shows how.

What to keep that people forget

Leaving things off does not mean stripping the page. MIT warns that a resume with no typos is still worthless without contact information. Harvard's own list of top mistakes names missing email and phone, spelling and grammar errors, and work that is hard to skim. Harvard also says to avoid gaps such as a missing summer. For how to handle one honestly, see the short job guide.

When cutting makes the page too thin, or too long

MIT gives physical limits that help you decide what stays. Use a conservative font no smaller than 10 point, margins of at least half an inch on every side, and bold only for key information or section headings. Stick to one page unless you have extensive experience or an advanced degree. If a resume overflows, do not shrink the type below that line. Cut the oldest or least relevant item first. MIT also suggests keeping a master resume with everything on it, then building a separate version for each position from the experiences that fit the posting. That gives you a place to keep the material you cut without sending it.

A quick pass before you send

Read your header and your last section. Anything there that tells the reader about who you are rather than what you can do is a candidate to cut. Check the opposite direction too: is your email address spelled correctly, and is the phone number one you answer? Ask one other person to read it, since both offices say small errors cost a candidate. Save the file, open it again, and look at the header once more. Then run the file through the bullet check.
