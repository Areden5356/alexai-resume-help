How to tailor a resume to a job posting, step by step

Updated 21 October 2026

Tailoring a resume does not mean rewriting your history. It means choosing which true things to show first and which words to use for them. A posting tells you what the employer is worried about. Your job is to show, quickly, where you have already dealt with that worry.

This is the method, in the order that wastes the least time.

Start with one master resume

Keep one long document that holds everything you have done: every job, club role, project and tool, with every bullet you have ever written. Nobody sees this version. Each tailored resume is a cut-down copy of it. Without a master you will keep rebuilding from memory and forgetting things that would have helped.

Step 1: Read the posting twice

On the first pass, read it the way an applicant does and notice how you feel about it. On the second pass, mark three things with a pen or a highlighter.

  • The job title, exactly as written.
  • Any phrase that appears more than once. If a posting says "customer onboarding" in the summary, again under duties and again under requirements, that phrase matters to them.
  • The first two or three items under requirements or qualifications. UVic's career services notes that employers often list their most important qualifications near the top.

Skim the paragraph about company culture. It rarely tells you what to put on a resume. Purdue's writing lab says you can echo a company's goals in your letter, but only if your experience supports them.

Step 2: Match each ask to evidence you actually have

Make two columns on a scrap of paper. On the left, write what the posting asks for. On the right, write where you have done it, with a real example. If the right side is empty, leave it empty for now.

Example, invented for illustration

Posting asks for: "Build weekly dashboards in Tableau."

Evidence: "Built a weekly sales tracker in Excel for my store job. Never used Tableau, but I took a data visualization course and used charts in the final project."

Posting asks for: "Strong written communication."

Evidence: "Wrote the weekly newsletter for the marketing club. Wrote the report for the survey project."

This table is the real work. Everything after it is formatting. It also shows you honestly where you are strong and where you are not, which is useful before you write the cover letter.

Step 3: Reorder, then cut

Inside each job or project, put the bullets that answer the posting first. Someone skimming sees the top of a section first. If the posting is about data and your best data bullet is third in the list, move it up.

Then cut. A bullet that has nothing to do with this job still costs the reader a few seconds. If you are a student with a one-page resume, cutting two weak bullets leaves more room for the three strong ones. If you must keep everything for a reason, shorten the unrelated bullets to a single line.

Step 4: Borrow the posting's words, where they are true

A reader checking your resume against the posting will spot the posting's own words fastest. If you did the thing but described it in different words, switch to theirs.

Example, invented for illustration

Helped people at the front counter and fixed problems with orders.

Posting uses: "customer support", "order issues".

Reworded: "Handled customer support at the front counter, including order issues."

The second version says the same thing you did. It just says it in a form that matches what the reader is looking for. If you never handled order issues, the second version would be a lie, and the first would be the only honest one.

Step 5: Adjust the top and the skills list

If you have a summary at the top, rewrite it to name the role you are applying for and the one or two strongest matches from your table. If you do not have a summary, you do not need one. A skills section is the place to list tools and methods the posting names, but only the ones you can talk about for two minutes without bluffing.

Step 6: Deal with the gaps out loud

Look at the empty rows in your table. For each one, you have three honest options. You can leave it off and let the rest of the resume carry you. You can mention related experience in plain terms, such as a course, a project or a self-taught tool. Or you can address it in the cover letter. What you cannot do is add the missing skill to the resume as if you had it. Interviewers can ask a follow-up about any line, including the one you added last night.

One missing requirement rarely ends an application. A resume that claims something you cannot explain often does.

Step 7: Check, save, and label

Run the tailored text through the free keyword check with the posting. It shows which of the posting's terms appear and which do not, and it runs in your browser without uploading anything. Treat a missing term as a prompt to ask yourself whether you have that experience. Do not treat it as a to-do list.

Save the file with your name and the company or role in the filename, for example Jordan_Example_Resume_Marketing_Analyst.pdf. Keep a copy of the posting too. Postings disappear when the job is filled, and you will want the text before an interview.

Things to avoid

  • Pasting the posting into your resume. A reader can tell, and it adds claims you have not backed up.
  • Hidden text. White text on a white background, tiny text and similar tricks are easy to find, and they look like an attempt to fool the reader.
  • Changing facts to fit. Dates, titles, grades and numbers stay as they are. Wording can change, facts cannot.
  • Over-tailoring. If it would take you an hour to change a resume for a posting you are lukewarm about, use your best general version and spend the hour on the postings you want most.

Honest limit: tailoring improves how clearly you show a fit. It does not create one, and it cannot make an employer interview you. Nobody can promise that.
