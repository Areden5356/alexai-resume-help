Career change resume: use only the facts you have

Updated 21 October 2026

Changing fields puts a hard question on the page: what do you have that this new employer needs? Many people answer it by stretching. They borrow the new field's vocabulary for old work, or imply experience they lack. That is risky, and it is also unnecessary. You almost always have true, relevant facts. The skill is in finding them and stating them plainly.

Start from the posting, not your old title

Your old job title describes where you were. The posting describes what the new employer needs. Write down the requirements, then go through your past work and ask, for each requirement, whether you did anything like it. Not "did I hold that title" but "did I do that task." Purdue's writing lab makes a related point: review job ads to see what experiences employers want and emphasize those, and think about how to present seemingly unrelated experience in a way that shows its value.

Three kinds of facts that carry over

  • Tasks. Scheduling, tracking numbers, writing reports, handling complaints, training someone, running events. These appear in nearly every field.
  • Tools. Spreadsheets, a scheduling system, a point of sale system. Name the tool only if you used it.
  • Setting. Working with deadlines, with the public, with confidential records, in a regulated place. State the fact, not the adjective.

The limited-experience pattern from Purdue

For people with little directly relevant experience, Purdue's writing lab suggests two subcategories under a job: Responsibilities and Skills Learned. The second one is where transferable skills go, such as communication, following directions, and working in a professional setting. You can adopt the idea without the exact labels: after the plain duties, add a line or bullet naming what the work taught you that the new field uses, if you can back it up.

Be plain about what is new

You do not have to hide the change. A short summary at the top can say it outright: what you have done and what you are moving toward. Then the rest of the page is easier to read, because the reader knows why a restaurant manager is applying to an operations coordinator role. See the summary guide for how to keep that block short.

A cover letter has room to say more. The cover letter guide covers where to put the sentence that names the change and the reason for it, in your own words.

An invented example

The person and numbers below are made up.

Example, invented for illustration 
Old line (restaurant shift lead):
- Responsible for scheduling and customer service

Facts the person supplied:
- Built the weekly schedule for 9 staff in a shared spreadsheet
- Adjusted it when people called out, usually same day
- Handled customer complaints and logged repeat issues in a notebook

New line (applying for an operations coordinator role):
- Built the weekly schedule for nine staff in a shared spreadsheet
  and rebuilt it the same day when someone called out
- Logged repeat customer complaints by hand and raised the most
  common ones with the manager

Notice what the new lines do not say. They do not claim "process improvement" or "data driven decisions," because the person did not describe those. The facts are the same; the order and the emphasis moved toward the posting.

What to leave out

  • Skills you have only read about. If you took a short online course, say that, with its name. Do not list the tool as a skill at the same level as ones you have used for years.
  • Vocabulary you cannot define. If an interviewer asks what a term on your resume means and you cannot answer, take it off.
  • Implied titles. Do not call yourself an analyst because you used a spreadsheet. Keep the real title and describe the real work under it.
  • Padding with old jobs. Irrelevant roles can shrink to a line each.

Fill gaps with real work, not claims

If the requirements list something you lack, you have three honest options. Do a small, real piece of work: a spreadsheet for a club, a volunteer task, a class project. Take a course and name it as a course. Or leave the gap and say so in the letter, as the example in the cover letter guide does with a missing tool. A plain gap is easier to defend than a stretched claim, though I cannot tell you how any one employer will react.

Order and layout

Purdue notes that a tailored resume can lead with the most relevant experience even if that breaks date order, and that experience can be split into several sections. A career changer can use that: a section named "Relevant experience" followed by "Other work," with each job still carrying its real title, employer and dates. Keep the layout plain so people and software can read it; the formatting guide has the rules.

A checklist before you send

  • Every line is something you did, and you can talk about it for a minute.
  • The most relevant work is easy to find in the first third of the page.
  • The change of direction is stated once, plainly.
  • No tool or term appears that you could not explain.
