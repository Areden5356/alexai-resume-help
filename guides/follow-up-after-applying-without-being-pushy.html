How to follow up after applying without being pushy

Updated 21 October 2026

You applied, and now there is silence. Following up can help, because an application can sit unread for reasons that have nothing to do with you. It can also annoy people if it is repeated, vague or demanding. This guide gives a restrained approach. I could not find a reliable source for exact timing, so the intervals here are my suggestions, and an employer's own instructions always win.

First, read what the employer said

Check the posting and any automatic confirmation email. Employers often say when they will respond, or that they will contact only selected candidates, or that you should not call. If the posting says "no phone calls," do not call. If it names a date, wait until that date has passed before you write. This one step prevents most follow-ups that go wrong.

Decide whether to follow up at all

Follow up when you applied for a role you want, you have a specific contact or a monitored address, and the stated timeline has passed or there was no timeline. Skip it when the posting asks you not to, when you applied to a large pool through a portal with no contact listed, or when you have already followed up twice.

A simple schedule

  1. After applying: Keep the confirmation and note the date and the posting's wording.
  2. About a week or two after the stated date, or after a couple of weeks if none was given: send one short message.
  3. Another week or two later: if there is still no reply, one more short note is reasonable.
  4. After that: stop. Keep applying elsewhere. A third message rarely helps and can hurt.

Those intervals are my judgment. Hiring can be slow for ordinary reasons: approvals, holidays, other candidates. A short wait is normal and not a rejection.

Who to write to

Use the contact named in the posting. If none is named, the recruiting or careers address on the company's site is fine. If you met someone, such as at a career fair, and they gave you a card or said to write, write to that person and mention where you met. Do not guess at private email addresses or message people through channels that are not meant for it.

What a good message contains

  • A clear subject line with the role name, so it can be matched to your application.
  • One sentence saying you applied, for which role, and roughly when.
  • One sentence of continued interest that is specific to the role, not a general one.
  • One plain question or offer: whether there is an update on timing, or an offer to send anything else they need.
  • A thank you and your name. Nothing else.

Keep it under about 90 words. Do not repeat your whole pitch, attach new documents unless asked, or express frustration.

An invented sample

Example, invented for illustration 
Subject: Marketing Analyst Intern application, Jordan Example

Hello Ms. Alvarez,

I applied for the Marketing Analyst Intern role at Trailhead
Supply on October 3. I am still very interested, especially in
the weekly reporting work described in the posting.

Could you tell me whether the role is still open and when you
expect to contact candidates? I am happy to send anything else
you need.

Thank you,
Jordan Example

It names the role and date, adds one specific reason for interest, and asks one answerable question. A reader can respond in a single line.

What not to do

  • Do not write "I haven't heard back" in a tone that blames. Silence is usually process, not rudeness.
  • Do not send the same message through several channels at once.
  • Do not claim another offer to create pressure unless it is true, and even then think twice. If you have a real deadline from another employer, you can say so plainly and politely, since it is useful information for them.
  • Do not follow up on the same day you applied.
  • Do not write to several people at the same company about the same application.

After an interview

CareerOneStop advises sending a thank-you note within 24 hours of an interview; the thank-you note guide gives the parts and a sample. Mention one specific thing from the conversation and restate your interest. If they gave a decision date, wait until it passes before asking. If they said nothing, one polite check after about a week is reasonable.

Keep your own record

Note every application with the date, the role, the contact and any message you sent. A simple list in a spreadsheet is enough, and it keeps you from sending the same note twice. Check the employer's name and the role against the posting before each message; the free cover letter check looks for these in a letter in the same way.
