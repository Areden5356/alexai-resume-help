How to list projects and coursework on a resume

Updated 21 October 2026

If you have little paid experience, projects and coursework can carry a resume. They can also clutter one. The difference comes down to whether each item shows something the posting asks for, and whether you describe it with facts instead of course titles.

What the sources say, and what they do not

Purdue's writing lab says the education section should center on your highest degree and list the institution, its location, the graduation date, the degree, and any minors or double majors. It says a grade point average is optional. It does not address coursework, and its page on honors and activities does not cover projects, though it does describe a section for awards, memberships and leadership, built from a brainstormed list and trimmed to the most relevant items. The rest of this guide, including where projects go, is my practical advice.

Projects: when they earn a section

A project is a piece of work with a goal, a method and an outcome that you can describe. Class projects, club projects, personal projects and volunteer work all count if you did real work and can explain it. Give projects their own section when:

  • they are more relevant to the posting than your jobs, or
  • your paid experience is short, or
  • the posting asks for something you used in a project, such as a tool or a method.

Put the section near the top, under education, if it is your strongest material. Put it lower if your jobs are stronger.

How to write a project entry

Use the same structure as a job. A heading line with the project name, the context (class, club, personal) and the term. Then one to three bullets covering what you did, how, and what came out of it.

Example, invented for illustration 
Campus Coffee Survey, class project, Spring 2026
- Wrote 12 survey questions and collected 60 responses from
  classmates
- Compared answers by age group with pivot tables in Excel
- Summarized three findings in a five slide deck for the class

Every number here would have to come from the student: 12 questions, 60 responses, five slides. If the student does not remember a number, the bullet should drop it rather than guess. A project bullet with no numbers still works if it says what was done: "Collected survey responses and compared them by age group in Excel."

Say who did what on group work. "Built the survey and ran the analysis" is honest if you did those parts. "Led the team" is a claim you must be able to support.

Coursework: use sparingly

A list of course names tells an employer little, because titles vary and most are unfamiliar to outsiders. Two cases where listing courses helps:

  • The posting names a subject and you took a course in it, and you have nothing else to show for it yet. A short "Relevant coursework" line under your degree is acceptable.
  • The course produced something you can describe. Then it is a project, and it belongs in the projects section with bullets, not in a list of titles.

Keep a coursework line to four to six courses, chosen from the posting. A line of twelve titles is padding.

In progress

If you are taking a course now, say so: "Data Visualization, in progress, Fall 2026." Do not list it as completed. The example letter in the cover letter guide uses the same honesty about a course in progress.

Grades

Per Purdue, including a grade point average is optional, and it suggests leaving off one at or below 3.0, noting that omitting it can lead readers to assume it is low. Whether to include yours is your call. If a posting asks for it, give it as asked and report it accurately.

Where it goes on the page

  1. Heading with contact details.
  2. Education, with a coursework line if you use one.
  3. Projects, if they are your strongest material; otherwise after experience.
  4. Experience.
  5. Activities and skills.

Reverse the middle two if your jobs are more relevant. Keep the format consistent across sections: if dates are on the right for jobs, put them on the right for projects. See the formatting guide.

What to leave out

  • Class assignments you cannot explain in two sentences.
  • Tools you touched once, listed as skills. Mark such items as coursework use, or drop them.
  • Projects with no result you can state, unless the process itself is the relevant point.
  • Anything from high school once you have college work to show.
