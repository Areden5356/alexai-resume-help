Interview thank-you note: structure, timing and a sample

Updated 21 October 2026

A thank-you note after an interview is short and has a simple job. It thanks the person, shows you are still interested, and gives them one more reason to remember you. It does not need to be long or clever. This guide gives the parts, the timing and a sample, and separates what a source says from what I suggest.

What CareerOneStop says

CareerOneStop, a US Department of Labor sponsored site, advises sending a note after every job interview, no later than 24 hours afterward, even if the interview did not go well. It lists what the note can do: thank the interviewer for the opportunity, say you are still interested, briefly restate your qualifications and skills, mention relevant background you did not cover, follow up on websites, books, articles or contacts that came up, and note the date and time of any follow-up you agreed on. It says to keep the note brief, address it to the interviewer by name and title, and, if you met several people, send a separate note to each or one to a key person asking them to share it.

On delivery, it calls a handwritten or typed note sent by mail an excellent choice, and says email is acceptable if time is short. I found no source giving a word count or a subject line, so those are my judgment below.

The structure I suggest

  1. Greeting by name. Use the name and title as the person gave it. Check the spelling against their email or card.
  2. Thanks, with the role. One sentence that names the role and the day you met.
  3. One specific thing from the conversation. A topic, a question they asked, or something they said about the work. This shows you listened, and it is the part a template cannot supply.
  4. One reason you fit. CareerOneStop suggests restating your qualifications. Pick one, and tie it to what you just mentioned. If you forgot to say something relevant, add it here.
  5. Close. Say you are still interested, and mention the follow-up timing if one was agreed. Then your name.

My judgment is that 90 to 150 words is enough. Longer starts to read like a second cover letter.

An invented sample

Example, invented for illustration 
Subject: Thank you, Marketing Analyst Intern interview

Dear Ms. Alvarez,

Thank you for meeting with me on Tuesday about the Marketing
Analyst Intern role at Trailhead Supply.

I enjoyed hearing how the team tests subject lines and then
changes the next send. It is close to what I did for a campus
club, where I compared which posts got replies each week and
adjusted the next week's plan.

I forgot to mention that I built a weekly sales tracker in
Excel at my retail job. I would be glad to show it to you.

I remain very interested in the role, and I look forward to
hearing from you by the end of next week, as we discussed.

Sincerely,
Jordan Example

The people, the company and the work are made up. In a real note, every claim must match what you told the interviewer.

Where to send it

Use the address the interviewer gave you, or the recruiter who arranged the meeting, and send from an address that includes your name. A note sent to a general inbox may sit unread. This is practical advice, not something the source states.

Timing

Aim for the same day or the next morning, in line with CareerOneStop's 24 hour limit. If you cannot send a mailed note in that time, send an email first. If you want a handwritten note as well, mail it the same day, but do not let it replace a timely email.

More than one interviewer

Ask at the end of the interview for the names and the best address for each person, or ask the recruiter afterward. Send separate notes, and change the specific thing in each so the notes are not copies. If you only know one address, send one note to that person and ask them to share it, as CareerOneStop describes.

What to leave out

  • New requests, such as asking about pay in a thank-you note. Raise those through the process the employer set up.
  • Complaints about how the interview went. If something went poorly, a plain sentence of correction is fine, such as a fact you got wrong.
  • A pitch you already made at length. One reason is enough.
  • Typos. Read it aloud and check the interviewer's name and the company name twice.
