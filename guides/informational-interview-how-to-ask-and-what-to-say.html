Informational interviews: how to ask and what to say

Updated 26 October 2026

An informational interview is a short conversation with someone who already works in a job, company or city you are curious about. You are there to learn, not to ask for work. For a student or career changer it is one of the few ways to hear what a job is like day to day from a person who does it. This guide follows MIT's career office, which breaks the process into five steps, and marks where I add my own view.

Step 1: pick who to ask

MIT says to start with people you already know: family, friends, neighbors, professors, past coworkers. Any of them may work in a field you want to explore, or know someone who does. Alumni directories, LinkedIn and professional associations are the other places it names. My judgment: ask for names before you ask for a meeting. Someone who cannot help may still say who can.

Step 2: send a short request

MIT's checklist for the message is brief. Introduce yourself, say how you got their name, say you are researching the field and want advice, and ask for 20 to 30 minutes. It adds that the purpose is not to ask for a job or internship. Offer a choice of where to meet, such as their workplace, a phone or video call, or a public place like a coffee shop. Be clear, concise and courteous.

Example, invented for illustration 
Subject: Question about your work in supply chain

Dear Ms. Alvarez,

Professor Chen in the business school gave me your name. I am
a junior studying operations and I am trying to learn what
supply chain work looks like day to day.

Could I ask you a few questions for about 20 minutes, by
phone, video, or wherever is easiest for you? I am not asking
about openings. I would simply like your advice.

Thank you for considering it.
Sam Rivera

The names are made up. In your own message, use the real source of the introduction, or do not claim one.

Step 3: prepare as you would for a job interview

MIT recommends researching the organization and the occupation so your questions can be specific, and writing a list of open-ended questions that help you judge whether the career fits. It also says to decide what you want out of the meeting beforehand, since goals range from general career research to advice about a particular job. Open-ended means questions that cannot be answered with a yes or no. Three starters, which are mine and not MIT's:

  • What does a normal week look like in your role?
  • What did you wish you had known before you started?
  • Who else would you suggest I talk to?

Step 4: run the conversation

The meeting is more casual than a job interview, but MIT still asks for a professional impression. Arrive early, especially at a coffee shop where you need a seat. Lead the conversation by thanking the person first. Watch the clock and stay within the time you asked for. Send a thank-you email or note within 24 hours. The thank-you note guide has a structure that works here too.

Step 5: sort out what you heard

MIT lists reflection questions: What did you like? Did anything raise a new concern? How does this change your goals? Is there another job or employer to look into? What is your next step, and who else will you talk to? It warns against leaning too hard on the views of one or two people, so plan on several conversations. It also suggests a running record of who you met, when, what you discussed, and any further contacts they gave you.

If you get no reply

MIT does not say what to do when a request goes unanswered, so this part is my judgment. People are busy, and silence usually means a full inbox, not a refusal. One short, polite reminder after about a week is reasonable. After that, move on to the next name on your list. Do not turn the reminder into a request for a job, because you told them you wanted advice. If someone says they cannot meet, thank them and ask if they could suggest anyone else. A kind no is still a useful answer.

Where this meets your resume

These conversations often change what you put first. After two or three, reread the job postings you care about with the posting guide and see whether your real experience matches the words people used. Do not add a skill because someone mentioned it. Add it when it is true.
