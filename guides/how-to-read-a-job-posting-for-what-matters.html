How to read a job posting for what matters

Updated 21 October 2026

A posting is a mix of requirements, wishes, company talk and logistics. Applicants who treat every line as equally important write a resume that says everything and stresses nothing. This guide shows a way to read one so you can see what matters most and what you can honestly show.

What the sources say

A career services page at UVic tells applicants to save the posting, then make two lists: required qualifications, such as education, experience and licenses, and the skills and abilities the employer names. Next to each item you write how you meet it, and you highlight those matches in your resume and cover letter. It says that if a posting has many criteria, focus on the most important categories, and that employers often list the most important qualifications near the top.

Purdue's writing lab asks applicants to highlight the company name, the job title and any job number, the requirements, and the application requirements. It says to address each requirement with a concrete example from your past work, and to note required years of experience and any license or certification. It lists logistics to note too: the contact person, whether a resume, application or both is wanted, how to submit, the deadline and start date, pay, hours, travel and similar details.

None of those pages sorts requirements into "must have" and "nice to have", or says how many you must meet. Where I do, it is my judgment.

Step 1: save it

Postings are taken down. Copy the full text into a file with the date and the web address. You will want the exact wording for your resume, your cover letter and, later, your interview.

Step 2: note the logistics

Before the content, write down what Purdue lists: the contact person, what to submit and how, and the deadline. A strong application sent in the wrong format or too late does not get read.

Step 3: make two lists

Follow UVic. In one list, put what the employer requires, such as year of study, a degree, a license, or years of experience. In the other, put the skills and abilities named. Use the posting's own words.

Step 4: mark the emphasis (my judgment)

Two signals suggest what matters most. First, position: UVic notes that important items often appear near the top. Second, repetition: a word that appears three times is probably central to the job. The free keyword check counts repeated terms for you. Words such as "required" and "must" sound firmer than "preferred" and "a plus". That reading is my judgment, not a rule from the sources.

Example, invented for illustration 
Marketing Analyst Intern, Trailhead Supply (invented posting)
- Build weekly dashboards in Tableau       (top item, repeated: "dashboards")
- Run A/B testing on subject lines         (repeated: "testing")
- Pull data with SQL                       (named once, acronym)
Requirements: currently pursuing a degree; experience with SQL and Excel
Plus: Google Analytics

From that, a reader would guess that dashboards and testing are central, SQL and Excel are required, and Google Analytics is a bonus. That is a guess about emphasis. Only the employer knows.

Step 5: match evidence to each item

Beside each item, write the fact you have: a class project, a club task, a job. Both UVic and Purdue say to match requirements to concrete examples. If the only honest match is a small one, write the small one. Then look at your resume and ask whether a reader would find that fact in ten seconds.

Step 6: face the gaps honestly

You will have items with no match. UVic says that when you lack experience in an area, you should show your related competencies and explain why you fit. The honest version of that is to name what you have used that is close, and not to claim the thing itself. The tailoring guide covers the wording.

Step 7: decide whether to apply

I know of no reliable rule for how many requirements you must meet. Treat the lists as questions. If you can show evidence for the central items and the gaps are small or learnable, apply. If the central requirement is a license or degree you do not hold, read the posting again to see whether it is truly required, and ask the contact if one is named.

Things people skip

  • The company description. Purdue says you can echo the company's goals in your letter, but only if your experience supports it.
  • The job title and number. Use them exactly in your documents.
  • Details such as shifts, travel and a driver's license. They can decide whether the job works for you.
