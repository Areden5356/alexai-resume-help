Resume formatting that software and people both read cleanly

Updated 21 October 2026

Some employers collect applications in applicant tracking software. That software pulls the text out of your file and sorts it into fields: name, jobs, dates, schools. Other employers have a person open the file and read it. You usually cannot tell which you are dealing with, so the safest layout is one that survives both.

The good news is that a layout that software reads well is also easy for people to skim. The plain version is rarely the worse version.

What is known, and what is not

No outsider knows exactly how each employer's system behaves. They differ, they get updated, and employers configure them differently. Nobody can guarantee that a resume will pass "the ATS", and anyone who says otherwise is selling something. What can be said is that plainer files give software less to get wrong.

Purdue University's writing lab has a page on scannable resumes, meaning resumes that a computer reads with optical character recognition so employers can store and search them. Its advice is to keep the layout plain, use one common font (it gives Times New Roman as the example), leave out images and tables, and align text to the left. The page does not mention applicant tracking systems, so read it as an older, related source and not as a description of any current employer's software.

The rest of this guide follows the same plain-layout idea. The specific failure modes below, such as columns being read straight across and headers being skipped, are commonly reported but I could not confirm them in a primary source, and they vary by system. The advice on section names and dates is my judgment.

The rules, with reasons

Use one column

Two-column templates look tidy, but text extraction can read across the page instead of down each column, so a skill from the sidebar lands in the middle of a job description. A single column reads top to bottom, which is also how a person reads it.

Avoid tables, text boxes and graphics

Tables and text boxes are the usual cause of scrambled order. Icons, skill bars and logos add nothing a reader can use, and text that is part of an image cannot be extracted at all. If a template needs a table to hold its shape, pick another template.

Keep contact details in the body

Some software may skip page headers and footers. If your phone number and email sit in the header area, they may not be picked up. Put them in the first lines of the page itself.

Name sections the usual way

Experience, Education, Skills, Projects. If you want to say "Work Experience" or "Relevant Experience," that is fine. "My Journey" is not, because a system or a hurried reader will not know what it is.

Write dates the same way every time

Pick one style, such as "Aug 2024 to May 2025" or "08/2024 to 05/2025", and use it for every entry. A mix looks careless and gives a parser more than one pattern to guess at. Include the month for jobs. For a degree, the graduation month and year is enough.

Use real bullets and normal fonts

Use the bullet style built into your word processor, or a plain hyphen. Skip decorative symbols. Purdue's page suggests dashes over bullet symbols for scanned documents, which is a sensible fallback if you want to take no chances. Stay with a common font such as Arial, Calibri, Times New Roman or Georgia, at a readable size.

Keep the file simple

Follow the application's instructions about file type. If it does not say, a Word file or a PDF made by exporting from a word processor is a fair choice. A PDF made by taking a picture of a page is not, because there is no text in it. Name the file with your own name, such as Jordan_Example_Resume.pdf, not resume_final_v3.pdf.

A free test you can run in a minute

Open your resume file. Select everything, copy, and paste into a plain text editor such as Notepad or TextEdit in plain text mode. Then read what you pasted.

  • Is everything there, in the order you would read it?
  • Did your contact details come through?
  • Do bullets show up as bullets, or as odd characters?
  • Is each job's title, employer and dates still together?

If the pasted text is jumbled, software is likely to be confused too. If it is clean, you have lost nothing and learned something. You can also paste that same text into the free keyword check, which reports on headings, bullets, dates and contact details, and runs entirely in your browser.

Example, invented for illustration

A clean paste of one entry:

Campus Outfitters, Sales Associate
Aug 2024 to Present
- Helped customers choose gear and ran the register on weekend shifts
- Tracked weekly sales in an Excel sheet and shared totals with the store lead

A scrambled paste from a two-column template, where the sidebar skills have landed in the middle of the entry:

Campus Outfitters, Excel
Sales Associate Canva
Aug 2024 to Present Mailchimp
Helped customers choose gear

Fancy layouts: when are they fine?

If you are handing a resume directly to a person at a career fair, or emailing it to someone you will talk to, a designed layout is a reasonable choice. For an online application form, assume the file will be processed and keep it plain. A good habit is to keep two versions: a plain one for applications, and a designed one for handing out, with identical content.

Length

Purdue's page on length says new graduates and entry-level applicants usually need one page, and that some have enough relevant experience for two. It also advises asking an employer whether two pages are accepted before you send them. Do not pad to reach a page.

Honest limit: plain formatting removes common causes of a bad read. It does not decide whether you are hired, and it does not tell you how any particular employer's software ranks anyone.
