LinkedIn headline and About section: a plain structure

Updated 21 October 2026

Your LinkedIn headline and About section are the two places where a stranger decides whether to read the rest. Neither needs to be clever. They need to say who you are now, what you are aiming at, and one fact that backs it up. This guide gives a structure for each, from the little that authoritative sources say plus my judgment, which I mark.

What the sources say

LinkedIn's own help page says the headline is generated from the positions you add, and that you can rewrite it to highlight an area of expertise or let your personality come through. It says the About section should convey your mission, motivation and skills, and suggests one or two paragraphs, or bullet points if paragraphs are not comfortable. It also says to include work and education that fits your current career goals, and that a good profile is complete, current and representative of you.

A career office at MIT adds advice for students. Keep the headline simple to start, such as a student or an aspiring professional in a field. Open the About section with one line about yourself, like the start of a cover letter, then add interests and goals. It also describes the profile as a keyword bank that recruiters search, and suggests taking keywords from job descriptions you are interested in. If you have little experience, it says at minimum to add what is on your resume.

Neither page gives a formula, a character count or a length in words, so those that follow are my judgment.

The headline: three parts

The default headline is your current title and employer. For a student that may be only a school name, which tells a reader nothing about what you want. A structure I suggest:

  1. Who you are now. Student, graduate, volunteer coordinator.
  2. The field or role you are aiming at. Use the words job postings in that field use.
  3. One thing that backs it up. A tool you have used, a project, a language.
Example, invented for illustration

Before: Student at a community college

After: Business student aiming at marketing analysis | Excel and survey projects | Spanish speaker

Write two or three versions and keep the one you can explain in a single breath. Do not claim a title you do not hold. "Aspiring" or "aiming at" is honest and still carries the search words.

The About section: four short parts

LinkedIn suggests one or two paragraphs. Here is how I would fill them, in order.

  1. One opening line. What you do or study, and what you want next. This is the line MIT compares to the start of a cover letter.
  2. Two facts. A project, a job or a class result, each with what you did. Use facts that are on your resume, so the two agree.
  3. What you are looking for. The kind of role, and one reason you like it.
  4. How to reach you. Say a message on LinkedIn is welcome. Leave your phone number and home address off a public page.
Example, invented for illustration 
I am a business student looking for a marketing analysis
internship for next spring.

This fall I wrote a 12 question survey for a class project,
collected 60 responses and compared them by age group in Excel.
I also keep the weekly sales tracker for a campus bookstore.

I like the part of marketing where a question gets answered
with a number. I am glad to hear from anyone doing that work.

Every number in that example is invented. In your own text, every number must be yours.

Keywords: borrow from postings, with care

Open three or four postings for the role you want and note the words that repeat. Use the ones that are true for you. The free keyword check does this count for a resume and a posting, and the same list works for a profile. Copying terms you cannot back up is the common mistake, and it can come up in an interview.

Keep it consistent with your resume

Employers may compare the two. Dates, titles and employers should match. The profile can be warmer and a little longer than the resume, but it should never claim more.

Then read it as a stranger would. If someone saw only your headline and the first two lines of the About section, would they know what you want and what you can do? If not, move your aim to the top, and cut whatever comes before it.

What to avoid

  • Words about yourself with no fact behind them, such as "passionate" or "results-driven".
  • A copy of your resume bullets pasted in whole. Summarize instead.
  • Skills you have never used.
  • Personal contact details on a public page.
