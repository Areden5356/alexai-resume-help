Common resume mistakes to remove before you apply

Updated 21 October 2026

These are the problems I would fix first on a student or early-career resume. They are all things you can change in an evening. None of them requires new experience. I have ordered them roughly from most to least damaging, but that ordering is my judgment, not something measured.

1. Duty lines instead of what you did

"Responsible for customer service" and "Duties included answering phones" describe the job description. A reader can guess them. Replace each with a line that says what you did, how often, and for whom. The guide on turning duties into bullets shows the method, using only facts you have.

2. Typos and inconsistent details

One typo can be forgiven. Three make a reader wonder how careful you are. Beyond spelling, look for mismatches: "Co-ordinator" in one place and "Coordinator" in another, a month written out in one entry and numbered in the next, a job title that differs between your resume and your cover letter. Read the resume out loud, slowly, from the bottom up. Reading backward makes you see words instead of skimming.

3. Mixed tenses

Past jobs in past tense, your current job in present tense. "Manage the schedule" under a job that ended last year reads as a slip. Check the verb at the start of every bullet.

4. Skill bars, stars and percentages

A bar showing "Excel: 80 percent" tells no one anything. Eighty percent of what? Instead, name the skill and prove it in a bullet: "Used pivot tables to compare survey answers by age group." If you do want to signal level, use words a reader can interpret, such as "coursework" or "daily use", and only where it is accurate.

5. A generic objective

"Seeking a challenging position where I can grow my skills" says nothing about you and could be on anyone's resume. Delete it, or replace it with a two-line summary that names the kind of role you are applying for and your best matching experience. If you have nothing specific to say, leave the space for experience.

6. Every job given equal weight

A resume should not be a diary. If you are applying for a data role, a summer of lifeguarding deserves a line or two, not the same space as your statistics project. Put the experience that is most relevant to this posting first, and cut or shorten the rest. See the tailoring guide for a way to decide.

7. Contact details in the page header, or missing altogether

Some software can skip page headers, and a reader should not have to look for how to reach you. Put your name, email, phone and, if you have a tidy profile, a link at the top of the page itself. Use an email address that looks professional, ideally a variant of your name. Check that any links you include actually work.

8. A layout that fights the reader

Tiny margins, a font so small that you squint, ornamental dividers, two columns, text boxes. If you cut content to give the layout some air, the resume gets better, not worse. For the software side of this, see the guide on formatting that parses cleanly.

9. A wall of text

Paragraphs of eight lines are skipped. Break experience into short bullets of one or two lines each. If a bullet runs past two lines, it is probably two points joined together. The free keyword check flags bullets over thirty words, and also tells you how many of your bullets contain a number, which is a quick way to see whether any of them say how much.

10. A careless file name and a stale file

resume_final_v3_NEW.pdf goes straight to a hiring manager's downloads folder and stays there. Use your name: Jordan_Example_Resume.pdf. Open the file one more time before you send it. Check that it is the version you meant, that the date range for your current job still says "Present", and that you saved it as the right file type.

Smaller things worth a glance

  • "References available on request" takes a line and tells the reader nothing new. Delete it.
  • A list of every software program you have ever opened. List the ones you can actually use and would be willing to be asked about.
  • A year-only graduation with no month, when the rest of the resume uses months. Pick one date style and use it.
  • Personal details such as a full home address are not needed on most applications. A city and state is enough.

Honest limit: fixing these makes your resume easier to read and harder to dismiss. It does not make you a better candidate than someone with more relevant experience, and no list of fixes can promise an interview.
