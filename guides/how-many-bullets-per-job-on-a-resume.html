How many bullets per job on a resume

Updated 21 October 2026

People ask for a number: three bullets, five bullets, never more than six. Ask a few career advisers and you will hear different answers, which is a clue that no rule exists. I have not found a source that sets one, and I will not make one up. What follows is a method for deciding for yourself, based on what the reader needs.

What the source I read does say

Purdue's writing lab describes each experience entry as the organization and its location, your title, the dates, and a description of your duties. It says standard practice is most recent first, and for a tailored resume, put the most relevant experience first even if that breaks the date order. It does not give a bullet count. It also suggests splitting experience into several sections when that helps, for example separating volunteer work from paid work.

So the structure is flexible. The count comes from the job and from the posting.

The rule I use: bullets follow relevance

Give the most bullets to the jobs closest to the posting, fewer to the rest. In practice that looks like this:

  • Your most relevant job: as many as you have good, distinct facts for, usually three to five. Stop when the next bullet repeats an earlier one.
  • A job with some overlap: two or three.
  • A job with little overlap: one or two, or a single line stating the title, employer and dates. Even an unrelated job shows you held a position and showed up. A line or two is enough.

Those ranges are my habit, not a standard. The point is the ordering, with the weight on what the reader cares about.

Test each bullet before you keep it

Ask four questions. A bullet that fails all four is a candidate to cut.

  1. Is it true, and can I discuss it? If an interviewer asks about it, can you talk for a minute?
  2. Does it show something specific I did? "Responsible for social media" says the title. "Posted three times a week to the club account" says an action.
  3. Does it matter to this posting? Look at the requirements and the repeated words. A bullet that touches a requirement stays. One that touches nothing in the posting goes first.
  4. Is it different from the others? Two bullets saying you helped customers is one bullet with two wordings.

An example of trimming

This job is invented. The original had six bullets for a role the posting barely touches.

Example, invented for illustration 
Before (retail job, applying for an analyst internship):
- Helped customers find products
- Ran the register on weekends
- Stocked shelves
- Tracked weekly sales in an Excel sheet for the store lead
- Answered customer questions
- Closed the store on Saturdays

After:
- Tracked weekly sales in an Excel sheet and shared totals with
  the store lead
- Ran the register and closed the store on weekend shifts

Two bullets now do the work of six. The sales tracking bullet matches an analyst posting. The second shows reliability. The cut bullets were true, but they repeated each other or did not matter here. For a retail posting, the first, second and fifth might be the ones to keep. The facts did not change, only what is relevant.

Length per bullet

Most bullets read well at one or two lines. A bullet running past three lines usually holds two facts that should be separate bullets, or one fact buried in detail. The keyword check flags bullets over 30 words and under 5, a rough prompt for the same thing.

Special cases

Short jobs

A job of a few weeks, such as a seasonal position, can carry one bullet. Do not pad it to match the others.

Old jobs

For jobs from several years back that are not relevant, one line is enough, or fold them into a short "Additional experience" group at the bottom.

Overlapping roles

If you held two roles at one organization, list the organization once and the roles under it, each with its own bullets.

No real experience yet

Then bullets come from classes, clubs and projects, and the same tests apply. See the guide on listing projects and coursework.

The two-minute pass

Highlight the posting's requirements. Under each job, mark the bullets that touch one. Count what is left. If your most relevant job has two marked bullets and your least relevant has five, move weight toward the first: add facts you left out, cut the unrelated ones. Then reread the page for repeated opening verbs and trim until each bullet earns its line.
