Answer behavioral interview questions with the STAR method

Updated 26 October 2026

Some interview questions ask for a story. "Tell me about a time you worked on a team." "What is a project you are proud of?" "Tell me about a time you failed." These are behavioral questions, and they are easy to fumble if you meet them cold. This guide covers a structure for the answer, how to split your time, and how to prepare. It leans on one university career office's page, and I label my own judgment where I add it.

What the question is really asking

MIT's career office describes behavioral interviewing as a way to measure a person's past behavior as a predictor of future results. The employer wants a specific example of you doing the thing. A few of the typical questions can be answered with a plain yes or no, such as "Do you have experience solving complex problems?" MIT's advice is to treat those as "yes, and": say yes, then give the example that shows it.

The four parts

The same office recommends the STAR formula. Here is each part, with the share of your answer it suggests.

  • Situation, about 20 percent. Give enough context that the listener follows the story. They do not need every detail.
  • Task, about 10 percent. Say what you were responsible for or what the goal was.
  • Action, about 60 percent. Say what you personally did, and point to the skills the question asks about.
  • Result, about 10 percent. State what happened. Use a number if you honestly have one, and say what you learned.

MIT says those shares are a guide, not a target to hit exactly. The point is that most of the answer should be your actions.

Four habits that make an answer better

  • Say "I" where it was you. On a team project it is tempting to say "we" all the way through, and then the interviewer cannot tell what you did. Describe your own part in the outcome.
  • Be specific. A general answer teaches the interviewer nothing. Name the tool, the task, the amount.
  • Stay with what happened. MIT warns that bending the truth can read as insincere and may not match your resume or what a reference says. It also means a true story is easier to remember under pressure.
  • Tie it to the job. Reread the posting before you choose a story. If the role involves teamwork or supervising, expect questions about it.

An invented example

Example, invented for illustration 
Q: Tell me about a time you took the lead.

S: In my second year, our club's fall sign-up table kept
running out of forms and the line backed up.
T: As treasurer, I was asked to fix it before the next event.
A: I counted how many forms each earlier table had used,
printed that many plus extra, and set up two lines, one for
new members and one for returning ones. I wrote a one page
checklist for the volunteers.
R: Nobody waited more than a few minutes at the next event,
and the club kept the checklist for later years.

That answer spends most of its words on the actions, names a role, and ends with an outcome that does not need an invented statistic.

Common slips

MIT's own sample makes the point with a tutoring story. A bare "I tutored kids in math" is a fact, not an answer. Wrapped in a situation, a task, what the student did and what changed, it becomes evidence. The usual slips are a long setup with a thin middle, a story where the speaker's name never appears, and an ending that just stops. If you run out of time, cut the setup first and keep the actions and the result. A short, true answer beats a long one that wanders, and the interviewer can always ask a follow-up question.

How to prepare

You cannot predict every question. MIT's suggestion is to prepare three to five stories, using notes or a simple outline, and expect each to fit more than one question. A story about leading a study group might answer questions about leadership, communication, and time management. To choose stories, read the posting and notice which verbs it repeats.

MIT also advises against memorizing a script. A memorized answer is harder to adjust to the actual question and can sound unnatural. My own judgment: write the story as five or six short bullet notes, say it aloud twice, and stop there.

After the interview

MIT suggests writing down what went well and what to change, and emailing a thank-you within 24 hours. The thank-you note guide covers the parts of that note. If a story depends on a number you do not have, the bullets guide shows how to find true facts to use in its place.
