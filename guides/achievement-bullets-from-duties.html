Turn job duties into resume bullets using only true facts

Updated 21 October 2026

"Responsible for social media" tells a reader that a task existed. It does not say what you did, how often, for whom, or what changed. A duty line describes the job. A good bullet describes your part in it.

The usual advice is to add numbers. That advice goes wrong the moment someone invents one. This guide works the other way round: collect the facts you have, then build the bullet from them. If a fact is not in your notes, it does not go on the page.

The shape of a good bullet

Most strong bullets have three parts, in this order: what you did (a verb), the thing you did it to or for (the object, with scope), and what came of it (the result), if there was one. Not every bullet needs all three. A bullet with a clear verb and a clear scope is already better than most.

Step 1: Write down facts, not sentences

Before writing any bullet, answer these questions about the role in a scratch file. Short answers are fine. "Don't know" is a legitimate answer.

  • What did I do, in plain verbs?
  • How many, how often, how long? (Posts per week, customers per shift, people on the team, size of the budget.)
  • Who was it for? (Customers, a manager, a club of forty members.)
  • What tools or methods did I use, by name?
  • What changed afterwards, and how do I know? (A number that went up, a process that got faster, a thing that stopped going wrong, someone who thanked me in writing.)
  • Did anyone ask me to do it, or did I start it?

Step 2: Build the bullet from the notes

Example, invented for illustration

Duty line: Responsible for the club's social media.

Facts the person wrote down: posts on Instagram three times a week; fall semester; followers went from 400 to 650 by the end of the term; she checked the numbers every Friday; the club had 40 members.

Bullet: Posted three times a week on the club's Instagram account during the fall semester; followers rose from 400 to 650.

Every part of the bullet comes from a line in the notes. Nothing in it is a guess. That is the whole method.

When you have no numbers

Many real jobs do not hand you numbers. A student who worked a front counter may not know how many customers they helped. You still have options that are honest.

Use scope instead

"Ran the register on weekend shifts" is specific. It says what, and when. "Trained two new hires on the point-of-sale system" is a count, and you know it without needing a report.

Use a plain outcome

If something improved but nobody measured it, say what changed without inventing a size. "Rewrote the closing checklist so the evening shift stopped missing the freezer log" has no number and is still a result.

Use named tools and methods

"Tracked weekly sales in an Excel sheet and shared totals with the store lead" tells a reader that you can work in a spreadsheet and that someone relied on what you produced.

Skip the bullet

If you cannot say anything specific about a task, it may not belong on the resume. A short list of strong lines beats a long list of vague ones.

Before and after, with the facts shown

Example, invented for illustration

Helped customers at the front counter.

Facts: ran the register on Saturdays and Sundays; handled returns; spring term.

Bullet: Ran the register and handled returns on weekend shifts during the spring term.

Example, invented for illustration

Wrote reports for my class project.

Facts: survey of 60 respondents; summarized in a slide deck; used pivot tables to compare by age group.

Bullet: Summarized 60 survey responses in a slide deck, using pivot tables to compare answers by age group.

Estimates and rounding

An estimate is acceptable if it is honest and you can defend it. "About 20 people per shift" is fine if that is a fair account. "Increased sales by 30 percent" is not fine if you made the percentage up. A useful test: could you explain, in an interview, exactly how you arrived at the number? If the answer is "I thought it sounded good," delete it.

Start with strong, plain verbs

Begin each bullet with a verb that says what you did: built, tracked, trained, wrote, scheduled, organized, handled, ran. Use past tense for past jobs and present tense for your current one. Avoid starting a whole section of bullets with the same word. If three bullets in a row begin with "Helped", at least one of them is probably hiding a more specific verb.

Avoid "responsible for" and "duties included". They describe the job description, which the reader can guess, instead of what you did.

If you use an AI tool for this

AI writing tools are good at phrasing and bad at knowing what is true. If you paste in "Responsible for social media" and ask for something stronger, you may get back a line with a percentage in it that nobody measured. Give the tool your fact notes, not just the duty line, tell it to use only those facts, and read every number in the output against your notes. That is also how I work on each pack: the facts come from the customer, and anything missing becomes a question to them.

Honest limit: a well-written bullet cannot make a small experience bigger than it was, and it should not try. Its job is to show what you did clearly.
